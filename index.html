Agustina Martínez

PhD Candidate in Economics

School of Economics, University of Bristol

I am a PhD candidate in Economics at the University of Bristol and I teach on the BSc in Economics and Data Analytics at the University of Leicester. My research studies how public demand shapes firms' employment decisions and how economic conditions and digital communication shape political discourse and polarization. I combine administrative and unstructured data with machine learning and causal inference methods.

Research interests: labour economics, public procurement, political economy, text as data, applied econometrics.

Research

Job Market Paper

  • Institutional Demand Stabilization and the Composition of Employment

    Solo-authored

    This paper studies how public procurement shapes firms' choice between permanent and temporary employment. I develop a dynamic firm-level model in which procurement contracts generate a persistent backlog of public demand and firms face asymmetric costs of adjusting permanent and temporary labour. The model shows that the effect of public demand on the permanent share of employment is amplified when private demand is more volatile and when adjustment costs are more asymmetric.

Working Papers

  • The Power of Words

    With Luis Menéndez and Henry Redondo

    This paper examines the rise of Vox in Spain through the interaction between local labour market conditions and political discourse. We combine a shift-share design with natural language processing of congressional speeches, party communication and Facebook posts, and exploit the timing of CIS Barometer interviews to study how political messaging shifts the salience of issues among citizens.

  • Hate in the Tropics

    With Diego Marino-Fages

    This paper studies how political leaders shape citizen discourse online, using the 2018 Brazilian presidential election and the rise of Jair Bolsonaro to examine changes in hate speech on social media across municipalities.

  • Raise Your Voice!

    Solo-authored

    This paper studies peer effects and network structure in online political expression, using the abortion-rights debate in Argentina on Twitter to distinguish echo chambers from other forms of network segregation.
